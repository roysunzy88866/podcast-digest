全球最大的全远程公司[[Deel|Deel]]做跨境雇佣和发薪，四年间年经常性收入从约2.5亿美元涨到15亿美元，还持续盈利近三年。

负责产品的高级副总裁[[Aaron Goldsmid|Aaron Goldsmid]]曾任职Amazon、Facebook和Twilio。

这期访谈里，他讲了一个很多公司还没想明白的问题：当人手不再是产出的衡量标准，公司该怎么管理、怎么挣钱。

## 人数为什么不再是好指标了？

Deel的研发团队约1700人，每天还在新招一两个工程师，产品团队约120人。但Goldsmid说，他已经不太看这些数字了。

过去，团队人数是复杂度、产出和范围的很好参照。**过去18个月，这个参照失效了**。

他现在只关心一件事：这些人是不是被充分用到，有没有资源把事情做完。他也不再谈产品经理和工程师的比例 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="06:15" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="R&amp;D is probably around 1,700, and that is almost certainly a wrong number, because we're hiring an engineer or two a day. Product is probably around 120, mixed up with a bunch of things between operations and so on. But the reality is, I actually don't pay a lot of attention to the count anymore. It used to be that those were incredibly good proxies for complexity, for output, for scope. But in the last 18 months, those proxies are just not valid anymore. So I end up focusing on how leveraged these people are. I don't talk about the PM-to-engineering ratio anymore. It's just, are these people fully loaded, and do they have the resources to be able to fully execute?" aria-label="回原文"></button>。

他的管理方式是双周业务评审：工程、产品、设计坐在一起，只谈实际工作，不许做漂亮演示。

团队可以意见不合，但必须信息对齐，分歧不能超过两周。

有意思的是，他连夜做了一个小机器人，读完全部评审记录后预测他会问什么问题——结果发现自己极其可预测。

他有点失望，但这个工具能让一线产品经理提前想好答案，省掉事后懊悔的循环 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="08:00" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="Most of that is really making sure you're close enough to the work on a regular basis that you can see. Almost every product team does a business review every two weeks, where it's engineering, product, design, and sometimes ops and sales, at the line level for that team. Their leadership, sometimes one above that, sometimes my level, and it's making sure they're going through the weeds: what's happened in the last few weeks, what shipped, how's the planning, how are things going, where are the blockers? It's not a time for pretty presentations. It's a time for the real work, and the only time that meeting goes well is when the team is aligned. They don't have to agree, but they have to be aligned. We may have two very different opinions on this topic, but there's no daylight between us on what those opinions are. We may not agree, but we all understand where everything is. What that forces us to do, especially as teams get bigger and the guilds (engineering, product, design) become identities in themselves, is they can never diverge for more than two weeks. They can drift, but within two weeks they have to come back and be on the same page. The other thing is it eliminates most of the pairwise conversations. I'm giving the product folks some advice, someone else is giving the design folks or the engineering folks advice, and even if we think we're aligned, we're saying it in slightly different ways, and it creates drift. Because when the organization gets to a certain scale, you're not in the room for every decision, so you need to make sure the decisions are good. Ideally, if you're doing your job well, the decisions are better when you're not there. The litmus test I hold for myself is: are my leaders asking all the questions in those reviews that I would, or better? If that happens, it's great, because I can step back and say, &quot;you guys got this.&quot; That's what I meant about imparting that culture down in the organization. I was actually talking with someone from another company last night, and I started building this overnight: a little bot that took all of our reviews and said, &quot;here are the questions I'm going to ask.&quot; Sadly, I'm wildly predictable, which I was kind of disappointed by, that I could be reduced to a soundboard, but apparently it's true. The idea is that I want the line-level PMs to be able to say, &quot;look, these are probably the questions you're going to get asked, make sure you have answers for them.&quot; I think that's really cool, because especially as I was coming up as a baby PM, you would obsess over this stuff: what am I missing, what am I not thinking about? After a review you'd beat yourself up, like, &quot;how did I not think about that?&quot; And as you grow up, you realize, of course these are the questions they're going to ask. Being able to short-circuit those cycles is going to be really cool." aria-label="回原文"></button>。

## 数据要重写成大模型能读的样子

Deel还建了一个[[语义层|语义层]]，让人可以直接用自然语言问数据问题。

这对一家全球分布的公司尤其重要：任何时刻都有同事在睡觉，以前要等对方醒来才能拿到数据，现在不用了。

他有个观察：这是搜索优化的新版本。

当年互联网几乎为搜索引擎重写了一遍，**现在所有内容、数据、知识都要为大模型重新组织**。

他们的做法是把几千名合规专家的知识放进一个标准化[[知识库|知识库]]——基于维基百科的结构，成为唯一可信来源，再让模型只从这个知识库里取内容 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="12:21" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="We've grown very, very quickly. You've heard the numbers, you've talked about the scale of engineering. Entropy is inevitable. One of our core promises is to deliver an all-in-one platform, where you don't necessarily have to take all the pieces, but there's a better-together thing. This is the classic G Suite or Office thing: what makes it wonderful is that these things naturally work together. In trying to figure out how we could make teams still move as quickly as possible but also deliver on that, there are two ends of the spectrum. One is everyone writes their own thing, which lets them move wildly quickly, you get a ton of endpoints. On the other side, everyone does the shared-service thing, but you always have to take a dependency. I tried to uncouple those two, because you want the upside of both but the downside of neither. So we let that happen at one layer and then used the dbt's of the world to create aggregation. There was an L2, which was how all the systems could talk to each other. Very quickly we realized that, both for our customers to access their own data and for us internally, because as you mentioned we're going to be one of the largest employers on the planet if you look at the folks through our payroll platform, we classically eat our own dog food. Almost every product we launch, we use internally first. We're always our first customer. So being able to say, &quot;these are the questions we need to ask of our organization&quot; for internal operational things, or trying to answer questions for our clients, is exactly the same problem. We built that semantic layer to say, &quot;how can I ask questions of all my data,&quot; knowing the surface area is so broad that no one's going to know what table it is. We got lucky, because we started doing this before the most recent evolution of the foundational or frontier models. The thing that makes that semantic layer good at explaining itself to a human, an internal resource or a customer, was actually really powerful for an LLM, because it's largely normalized. It looks like a spreadsheet, versus a complicated dataset. So the quality of response that comes out of it is incredibly good, which was a healthy reminder. We did this also with our knowledge base. The inputs you make matter. I've been talking about this for a couple of years: there's a new version of SEO. We almost had to rewrite all of the internet to work for search engines. We now need to think about all of our content, all of our data, all of our knowledge being consumed by these LLMs. We customized and tailored it to that. We've been doing a lot of that, especially with the corpus of global work and all the legislation and compliance rules. It's really powerful, because if you know you're writing for the LLM, the risk of hallucination goes wildly down, because it's consumable in a way the model expects. So it's much more predictable outcomes. Removing Room for Error in a Regulated Environment" aria-label="回原文"></button>。

好处是幻觉风险大幅下降：内容本来就用模型容易消化方式写的，输出就稳定得多。

## 小模型怎么打赢顶级大模型？

在这个自建知识库上，Deel用[[小语言模型|小语言模型]]做训练，效果至少和Sonnet一样好，质量正在逼近Opus——但响应快得多，成本只有零头 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="16:30" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="There are a few things we're doing, and we started this before LLMs were privacy-safe, where you had a real chance of it giving you someone else's data. So it was all at the infrastructure level. At the end of the day, the LLM is non-deterministic: you ask it a question a thousand times, it's not going to give you the same answer a thousand times. Maybe with some of the classifier models that's going to be a bit more predictable, and I'm really excited about that for our use cases. But it really is about structuring your input and making sure you know what corpus you can pull from. We have thousands of compliance experts in all of these markets. We've built, just organically, a knowledge base, and we've made an enormous effort to put this all in a standard wiki, based off Wikipedia, in a very easily accessible way. It's a source of truth. Because we created this corpus, that's what we focus the LLMs at. Now we're taking some small language models and training them on our own knowledge base and corpus. We're at least as good as Sonnet and quickly approaching Opus in terms of quality on a small language model focused on the corpus of global work and compliance. The upside is second-level responses, versus waiting five, ten, fifteen, twenty seconds, and a fraction of the cost. What we're really seeing is that the investment in that foundational work is allowing us to build a moat in an AI world, because we now have our own model and it's performing really well. It's also highly reliable, because it's being trained on a known-good set of data, versus being trained on the universe and then told, &quot;try to only look here, but we can't enforce that, and pretty please don't do other things.&quot; The Right Tool for the Right Job" aria-label="回原文"></button>。

> 【背景】Sonnet和Opus是Anthropic公司的大语言模型。

关键在于可靠：模型是在已知正确的数据上训练的，而不是「训练了整个互联网，然后恳求它别看别处」。

他还要求团队说清楚大模型不擅长什么。

表现最好的团队都了解工具的边界，知道哪类问题适合用模型解决、哪类不适合——就像扳手也能当锤子使，但你有时候就是需要一把锤子 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="18:08" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="There are two ways to think about this. One: I started asking teams, we love talking about the wins, &quot;I was able to make an LLM do this, the new model can now do that.&quot; One of the things I've really focused on asking my teams is: tell me the thing that didn't work. Tell me the thing the LLM sucks at. Because I find the teams that are performing really well understand where the edges are of the current model or approach. If you understand that edge, you can say this class of problems is really good to solve this way, and this class of problems is not. The thing I really like, and obviously it's the new hotness, the latest models, is that we were taking something that naturally outputs a narrative form, it writes back to you, and trying to use deterministic software to do that. Obviously the coding agents were creating deterministic software, but creating something where the output looks more consumable, rather than shoehorning computational data into this modality where narrative form comes out and then trying to recast it into computational data, the amount of error propagation and signal loss along that way is just wild. It was a square peg in a round hole. But now that we're starting to see things that output as computational data, or other models working on tabular data, it's speaking the right language. It's the right tool for the right place. Yes, you can technically use a wrench as a hammer, or a screwdriver as a hammer, but sometimes you just want the hammer. So it's allowing us to use the tools that are fit for the task, especially as folks get wildly excited. You just need to go to X on any day: every company is talking about how, &quot;look at all the things I was able to do with the new model.&quot; I joke about founders being like, &quot;I can now do 300% of our code using an LLM.&quot; That's more code than you. The math doesn't work, that's not how percentages work. But understanding where that edge is is what makes those teams incredibly comfortable, because there's so much noise in the system about what these things can do in theory but aren't great at yet. And the things they may be great at tomorrow, we're also finding cases where it was good at something and now it's not. There are regressions. There is drift. Understanding where that efficient frontier is is incredibly powerful for making sure teams use the tools in the right way. ## 1.4亿美元是怎么捡回来的？

Deel发布过一个数字：靠AI优化找到超过1.4亿美元的年经常性收入，相当于600人的工作量。主持人问具体怎么算出来的。

Goldsmid的回答是：**这是弯腰捡地上的零钱**<button class="pd-ts" data-t="21:17" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="It's picking up the change on the floor. In the before time, writing software, writing automation, was an incredibly expensive thing. Software was the last artisanal craft. Everything was handcrafted. You could look at a piece of code and know who wrote it. It was like people who built wagon wheels. And that has changed. So what happened is, when it was still that artisanal craft and a very precious commodity, there were so many aspects of the business that were still manual and didn't need to be. It wasn't because there wasn't a desire to automate, it was because there was no awareness that someone was manually moving this data from one system to another, or reformatting this spreadsheet every month, or &quot;I have to go log in to this one place and click this one button because it doesn't have an API.&quot; It just fell below the line. We hadn't gotten to it yet. What this enabled us to do, first, is that the list of things we were able to hit just got way, way deeper. The second unlock is we took it out of the hands of the engineers. How can people build these automations and workflows for themselves, not necessarily inherently technical resources, in a way that they feel really confident that their intention was captured and they could rely on the output, that it was as good as them? How would you do this if it was just humans? If you had an intern, you'd say, &quot;I have this thing I have to do every week, come shadow me, watch me do it, then show me how you do it, and I'll check the results.&quot; We built off exactly how humans interact. The product is called AKI, and that's how we built it. So how far down that list of wildly inefficient little things can you get? That includes the go-to-market function, the partnership function. It goes back to the marginal-user theory: who is the person who should be able to complete this task but is the least likely to be able to complete it? What's that one thing you need to fix, the button where you change the name, and all of a sudden they're successful? We were able to do that for a ton of operational things. If you think about what our day is, it's all operational stuff. A lot of my day is operational, your day is operational. So how much of that backlog can you get? The answer is, there's a lot there. While any one of those things may not feel like a big deal, and if it was a big deal we probably already would have automated it, they compound. The pennies add up, and all of a sudden they become big numbers. The second part is that it allows us to continue to scale at a different efficiency. The old scaling function was that complexity would go geometrically against a linear scale, or exponentially in terms of complexity. Now that looks to be more linear, because you have so much compute resource keeping the complexity down. Unlocking Non-Engineers to Ship" aria-label="回原文"></button>。他的比喻是，过去写软件是最后的手工艺——看一段代码就知道是谁写的。

因为软件太贵，公司里堆积了大量没人知道的手工活：

有人每月手动重排一张表格，有人因为某系统没接口而定期登录去点一个按钮。

这些事太小，不值得立项，甚至从来没进过待办清单。

AI改变了两件事：一是能处理的问题清单一下子深了好多层；二是把这些[[自动化|自动化]]从工程师手里解放出来。

他们的产品叫[[AKI|AKI]]，让非技术人员像带实习生一样手把手教系统：你做一遍，它看着学，然后做给你检查。

单个看都是小事，但复利累积，零钱变成了大数字。而且复杂度的增长曲线从几何级变回了线性。

## 坏主意变多了，但失败便宜了

这些自动化想法里确实有很多烂主意——Goldsmid直言，坏主意是真实存在的。但快速失败的成本已经低到不用心疼。

更妙的是，一批原本不好意思举手的人，现在能直接动手做那些我以为不值一提的事，里面藏着真正的钻石 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="25:01" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="Yes. And these were things that last year weren't even on the backlog, because nobody even thought to mention them: &quot;oh, I don't want to bother you with that, it's just the thing I have to do.&quot; All of a sudden it was incredibly powerful. To your point, there are some really terrible ideas. People say there are no bad ideas; there actually are bad ideas. But what's great is the cost of failing fast has gotten so low. You can have bad ideas, go, &quot;oh, that's not a good idea, we should make that go away,&quot; and then make it go away. You don't need to be precious about it. But in those bad ideas are a bunch of real diamonds, things no one ever would have thought of, because they didn't want to raise their hand and mention it. Because you can just do this now, it allows a class of people who wouldn't otherwise have raised their hand to advocate for this thing and make some really cool stuff, because everything is now safe enough to try. Build vs. Buy When Building Is Cheap" aria-label="回原文"></button>。

谈到自建还是外购，他引了一句拳王Mike Tyson的话：「每个人都有计划，直到脸上挨了一拳。」

一个周末就能搭出任何企业软件的顺路版本，**但维护才是真正难的部分**。

工程史的老教训——建容易、养难——会由新一代平民开发者重新学一遍。

## 快速增长和盈利，为什么能同时做到？

Deel盈利近三年，同时还在高速增长。Goldsmid解释：别的公司把增长还是盈利当二选一，**Deel问的是怎么盈利地增长**<button class="pd-ts" data-t="30:05" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="We have the benefit of truly incredible product-market fit, which is not just us, it's the work we're doing and the tailwind of a generational moment in terms of people thinking about global work, between Covid, between expansion, all of these things. 他的应对思路是回到本质：[[LLM|LLM]]本质上是在模仿人类行为，而底层业务流程——怎么发工资、怎么算税、怎么办签证——一百年没变过。

他坚持关键环节要有[[人在回路|人类在环]]：合规、工资发放、复杂的解约，不能拿来试错。

但人应该只做真正需要专业判断的部分——这样每个人都变成高杠杆的人才。

## AI取代的不是工作，是工作里最烂的部分

Goldsmid最广为流传的一句话是：AI不会取代你的工作，它取代的是你工作里最差的那部分 <button class="pd-ts" data-t="35:34" data-who="Aaron Goldsmid" data-en="The way I like to talk about it is: it's not going to replace your job, it's going to replace the worst part of your job. I wouldn't say, &quot;have it write your three-year strategy and take that as gospel,&quot; that'd be an objectively terrible idea. Not to say you can't have it craft some of it and be a thought partner. But have it approve the expenses. What value do I add there? &quot;Oh yeah, this was the right amount for this hotel.&quot; I'm sure I'm responsible about it. Yes, if something's crazy, send it to me. But for the most part, it's that level of judgment. &quot;Oh well, they were ten cents above the limit.&quot; Trying to do something in New York and trying to do something in Lisbon are not the same, so okay, we create one limit, it's ten cents, it's fine. 哪些事你既不从中获得价值、也不给它增加价值、但又必须完成？把这些交给代理。

比如审批报销：谁能从「确认酒店金额对不对」中获得价值？超出十美分就放行吧。

这种过去只能靠人做的小判断，现在计算机反而很擅长。至于让AI替你写三年战略并奉为真理——那是个彻头彻尾的坏主意。

## 本集带走

- 人数不再是产出的可靠指标，要看每个人是否被充分使用、有没有资源执行。
- 数据和知识要为大模型重写：结构化、可消化的输入，能把幻觉风险降下来。
- 在自有知识库上训练小模型，效果可逼近顶级大模型，成本和速度占优。
- 1.4亿美元来自自动化大量没人好意思提的小事——零钱会复利成大数字。
- AI取代的是工作中最无价值的部分；真正的判断力留给高杠杆的人。
